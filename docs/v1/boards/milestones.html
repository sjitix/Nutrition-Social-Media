The bar

What V1 means

V1 is the product a stranger can use end to end on their own phone, that is honest about every number it shows, and that we can put in front of an audience without a caveat. The walk-through that must work with no explanation from us:

01

Open it on a phone and understand what it is in five seconds.

02

Onboard, get a real week — fresh or meal-prep — macros on target, constraints respected.

03

Change it by talking to it, and watch the plan actually change, correctly.

04

Shop from it: aisle-grouped list, check-offs that persist.

05

Come back tomorrow and it is all still there.

Out of V1, deliberately: user uploads and creator tools (Phase 4), the workout vertical (Phase 5), and any social feed beyond Explore.  The three things that would make it not-V1: a constraint violation, a number that lies, and a dead end on a phone.

A7

The maths, proven exact

Every number the app shows is computed in one layer of pure functions — the one place where “perfectly” can be proven. Property tests for every law (2× an ingredient is 2× its calories; a cup is 16 tablespoons; the published Mifflin-St Jeor examples come out exactly; an allergen matches both ways and never inside another word). Every USDA entry checks its own maths. deriveMacros refuses an unknown ingredient instead of quietly skipping it.

Done 2026-10-03, and it found real harm. 83 laws over five areas: units, recipe macros, targets, the USDA table and allergies. Each was run against the code, and three skeptics judged every one that failed. 30 failed. Every real one is fixed, and every law is now a test.
  • Allergies were easy to lose. Ordinary ways of typing them (“peanuts.”, a line break, “dairy-free”, “severe peanut allergy”) lost the allergy, and the planner served the food. So did “prawns” meaning shrimp, and whey protein powder for a milk allergy. The worst was my own fix from the same afternoon: “fine with almonds but allergic to peanuts” was served peanuts.
  • Targets accepted nonsense, telling someone aged 500 that they burn −570 kcal at rest. Body stats outside the adult range are now refused, with the range given.
  • Units: “2 pieces” and “2 piece” weighed different amounts, and “2 (400 g) cans” read as 200 g. Seven recipes are re-weighed.
  • Five diet tags were wrong, raw shrimp had no vitamin B12, and the calorie check could not catch a wrong quantity. It has a ceiling now that can.

Then the fix itself was reviewed by eleven agents, and they found a hole in it: “I can eat anything without gluten” lost the allergy. That is closed too, with every reviewer's case kept as a test.

The automationDone. check:recipes and check:ingredients now run in the ship script and on every push to GitHub, so a new ingredient or recipe is verified every time, by default. That is what makes “expand by a lot” safe.

Expanding the library, and real-world products

You asked to expand the database a lot, and to think about an ingredients database wired to real retailer products. Checked against the code first, because the facts change the answer.

501

recipes in the library today

180

curated ingredients holding all of them up

0

ingredient ids — recipes point at free text

0

prices or products of any kind

The constraint is ingredients, not recipes. A new recipe may only use an ingredient already curated to a real USDA fdcId, because auto-matching is banned — it produced salmon fillet → Salmonberries, and shipping that means fabricated nutrition presented as real data. So "expand a lot" is a slow half (curate ingredients, hand-verified) and a fast half (write recipes over them). D5's parallel item exists to speed up the slow half without ever auto-accepting a match.
The three things in your commentWhere it landsWhy
Expand the library needs a number Proposed 180 → 400 ingredients, 501 → 900 recipes, judged by the export's Gaps sheet — any diet/slot cell under seven forces a week to repeat a dish. "A lot" with no number has no gate.
An ingredients database V1 · day 5 The layer exists; what it lacks is identity. Nothing can hang off a string — not a price, not a product, not an allergen flag. Cheap now, a migration of 900 recipes later.
Wired to Lidl-style products straight after V1 Additive once ids exist, a rewrite without them. It also needs a data source nobody has established yet — see below.
What the product layer would actually buy, and what it costs. It makes approxCost real — today it is a 1–3 integer that never exceeds 3, so the budget: high setting behaves identically to medium; it makes the grocery list shoppable with a true total; and it turns meal-prep's bulk-buy saving into a measured figure instead of a model. The cost: products map many-to-one onto ingredients, carry pack sizes (the gap between "buy 500 g" and "the recipe wants 80 g" is where the saving comes from), carry prices that go stale, and are per-store and per-country. A price must therefore show when it was last seen — in a product whose whole claim is that its numbers are real, a confidently stale price is the same class of failure as a hallucinated macro.

Researched 2026-10-02

Does Lidl actually have the data? No — but something better does

Lidl has no official public API. Every “Lidl API” on sale is a third-party scraper, which is itself the tell. The useful finding is that products and prices are two different problems with two different answers.

What we needSourceState, measured
Products
barcode, name, brand, nutrition		 Open Food Facts
ODbL, free, real API		 well covered Lidl's own-brands are there — Milbona 3,922 products, Italiamo 1,022, Combino 562. Barcode-keyed, which is exactly what an ingredient→product mapping needs.
Prices Open Prices
Open Food Facts' sister project		 thin and clumpy 578 Lidl stores in the database, but a 12-store sample gave a median of 4 prices per store (the mean of 59 is skewed by two enthusiast-covered shops). Growing — ML price extraction from shelf photos is funded.
Prices, commercial third-party scrapers $0.90–$2.99 per 1,000 products, some free tiers, 10+ EU countries. Cheap, fragile, and legally the nuanced one.
The legal shape matters more than the legality. With no official API, the commercial options are scraping by another name. EU law gives a database maker a right against extraction of a substantial part, and Germany's Federal Court of Justice reiterated protection against systematic scraping in a 2025 flight-price case. The consistent reading: a few hundred targeted lookups are low risk; mirroring a whole catalogue is not. So “pull the Lidl catalogue nightly” is the shape to avoid, and “look up the 200 things this library actually uses” is the defensible one — which happens to be all we need.
So the plan gets better, not worse. Map ingredients to products on Open Food Facts (free, open, already covers Lidl) — which needs only the identity from day 5. Hand-price the ~200 staples, each with a visible “priced on” date, so it works on day one and is honest about its own staleness. Then let the shopping trip improve it: the grocery list already has check-offs, and “tick it off, snap the price tag” feeds a real price back into both our data and the open commons. That is the same move this project already made with photography — contributions are the upgrade, not the threat — and it turns the weakest data dependency into the one asset that gets better with use.
Definition of done

How a day is judged

  1. The milestone's usable-on-its-own bar is met — not “the code is written”.
  2. The gate named in the row is green. Never push red.
  3. It is committed and pushed the same day — git log origin/main..HEAD reads empty.
  4. The daily-history entry is written: what was tackled, how much was solved, what else surfaced, and whether it goes on tomorrow or a later day.
  5. If the day moved a module boundary, the module map is updated in the same commit. A map that lags the code is worse than none, because it is believed.
